# Evidence

Owner of: what the design claims rest on, and which widely-repeated numbers are
not real.

The second list is the load-bearing half. A page that cites only good sources
gets the bad ones re-added by the next person who searches for them, because the
bad ones rank well and sound authoritative.

## Safe to cite

**Design quality is the most-cited credibility factor.**
Fogg, Soohoo, Danielson, Marable, Stanford & Tauber (2003), *How do users
evaluate the credibility of Web sites?*, Proceedings of DUX '03. 2,684
participants evaluating live sites; "design look" appeared in **46.1%** of
credibility comments, ahead of information structure and information focus.
Part of the Stanford Web Credibility Project, which ran three years across
4,500+ people. https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/997078.997097

Their working model is the useful part: **Prominence x Interpretation =
Credibility**. A trust signal counts only if it is noticed *and* then read
favourably, which is why a logo cloud nobody looks at scores zero.

**Above the fold is worth far more than below it.**
NN/g, *The Fold Manifesto*. Eyetracking across **57,453 fixations**: the 100px
just above the fold were viewed **102% more** than the 100px just below. NN/g
also names the **"false floor"** - a minimal, airy design that reads as the end
of the page and stops the scroll.
https://www.nngroup.com/articles/page-fold-manifesto/

**Minimalism has measurable characteristics, and measurable failure modes.**
NN/g, *The Characteristics of Minimalism in Web Design*. 112 sites analysed;
five traits present in 75%+ - flat patterns (96%), limited palette (95%),
restricted elements (87%), maximised negative space (84%), dramatic typography
(75%). The same study records that flat design "often fails to communicate which
elements are selectable or clickable", and that minimalism for its own sake does
not help users.
https://www.nngroup.com/articles/characteristics-minimalism/

**Credibility factors are stable over time.**
NN/g, *Trustworthiness in Web Design: 4 Credibility Factors* - design quality,
up-front disclosure, comprehensive and current content, connection to the rest
of the web. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/trustworthy-design/

**Progressive disclosure, as a method.**
Nielsen (2006), *Progressive Disclosure*. Two requirements worth quoting when
someone proposes hiding something: everything users *frequently* need must be
disclosed up front, and the affordance reaching the rest must be obvious and
correctly labelled. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/

**Why so many sites share one palette.**
Adam Wathan, creator of Tailwind, publicly regretted making every Tailwind UI
button `bg-indigo-500`, "leading to every AI generated UI on earth also being
indigo" (August 2025). A primary artifact, and the cleanest explanation of the
mechanism: defaults become training data, training data becomes the centre.

## Do not cite

**"Baymard found trust badges give a 23% higher conversion rate."** False. The
underlying study is a Google Consumer Survey Baymard ran over three days in
January 2013, n=2,510, asking which badge *felt* most trustworthy - Norton ~36%,
McAfee ~23%, TRUSTe/BBB ~13%. It is a perception survey, and Baymard explicitly
declines to infer conversion from it. The "23%" circulating as a conversion lift
is McAfee's share of a trust vote, relabelled. The related "147 sites, 15-30%
average lift" appears nowhere on baymard.com.

What Baymard does support is better for our purposes anyway: perceived security
comes from how a payment step *looks and behaves*, not from badges.

**"Progressive disclosure makes task completion 30-50% faster."** Not in
Nielsen's article. He mentions 46 web applications tested and says the technique
has proven useful; he publishes no such figure.

**Wynter's "5 bad words" as research.** It is a practitioner's opinion piece
with rewrites - no panel size, no method, no quantitative finding. Useful as a
heuristic, not as evidence.

**Any "best SaaS websites 2026" listicle.** Almost all are SEO content farms
recycling each other, several of them machine-written.

## Weaker, and labelled as such

A 2026 agency study (Digital Applied) reports named-customer proof at +22%, a
single testimonial at +14%, and logo strips at +8%, with a published method
(n=2,000 tests, 95% significance, minimum 1,000 sessions per variant). It is the
agency's own client portfolio, unpublished data, not peer-reviewed, and the
agency benefits from the conclusion. Directionally consistent with everything
above, so treat it as a hypothesis to test rather than a citation.

The frequently-repeated "users judge a site in 10 seconds" and "your value
proposition must be readable in 5 seconds" are heuristics. The 10-second figure
traces to old NN/g page-duration work; the 5-second rule is nobody's finding.
